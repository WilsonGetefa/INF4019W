QUESTION 3A: Modular Arithmetic
i) Modulo Notation/Operation vs. Congruence Modulo Notation
- Modulo Operation ($a \bmod n$): This is a binary arithmetic function that takes two integers, divides the first by the second, and returns a single, absolute remainder value as an element of the set $\mathbb{Z}_n$. For example, $7 \bmod 3 = 1$.
- Congruence Modulo Notation ($a \equiv b \pmod n$): This is a relational statement or equivalence property establishing that two distinct integers $a$ and $b$ leave the exact same remainder when divided by $n$. This can also be defined as $n$ evenly dividing their difference, written as $n \mid (a - b)$. For example, $7 \equiv 4 \pmod 3$.

ii) 3 Properties of Modular Arithmetic
   1. Compatible with Addition: If $a \equiv b \pmod n$ and $c \equiv d \pmod n$, then:
   $$(a + c) \equiv (b + d) \pmod n$$ 
   2. Compatible with Multiplication: If $a \equiv b \pmod n$ and $c \equiv d \pmod n$, then:
   $$(a \times c) \equiv (b \times d) \pmod n$$ 
   3. Reflexivity: Any integer is always congruent to itself under any modulus:
   $$a \equiv a \pmod n$$ 

iii) Modular Multiplicative Inverse
The modular multiplicative inverse of an integer $a$ modulo $n$ is an integer $x$ such that the multiplication of $a$ and $x$ is congruent to $1$ under that modulus:
$$a \cdot x \equiv 1 \pmod n$$ 
This inverse exists if and only if $a$ and $n$ are coprime, meaning their greatest common divisor is 1: $\gcd(a, n) = 1$.
iv) Value of -6 mod 10
To find a negative modulo value, add the modulus until the result is a positive integer between $0$ and $n-1$:
$$-6 + 10 = \mathbf{4}$$ 
v) Shift Cipher Method (Key = 7)
The alphabet is mapped directly to numerical values from $0$ to $25$ ($A=0, B=1, \dots, Z=25$). The modulus is $m = 26$.
- The Encryption Formula: $E(x) = (x + 7) \bmod 26$
- The Decryption Formula: $D(c) = (c - 7) \bmod 26$

Encryption Example (First Word: "LET")
- L ($x=11$): $E(11) = (11+7) \bmod 26 = 18 \rightarrow \mathbf{S}$
- E ($x=4$): $E(4) = (4+7) \bmod 26 = 11 \rightarrow \mathbf{L}$
- T ($x=19$): $E(19) = (19+7) \bmod 26 = 26 \bmod 26 = 0 \rightarrow \mathbf{A}$

The word "LET" encrypts into "SLA". The sender continues this calculation across the entire message string. To decrypt, the receiver reverses the shift by subtracting 7 from each ciphertext character numerical position modulo 26, successfully reconstructing the plaintext "LET".
vi) Euclidean Algorithm and Bézout's Coefficients for 40x + 64y = d
Step 1: Standard Euclidean Algorithm to find $d$ ($\gcd$)
   1. $64 = 1 \times 40 + 24$
   2. $40 = 1 \times 24 + 16$
   3. $24 = 1 \times 16 + 8$
   4. $16 = 2 \times 8 + 0$

The last non-zero remainder is the greatest common divisor:
$$\mathbf{d = \gcd(40, 64) = 8}$$ 
Step 2: Backward Substitution to solve for Bézout's Coefficients ($x, y$)
Isolate the remainders from our steps above:
- From step 3: $8 = 24 - 1 \times 16$
- From step 2: $16 = 40 - 1 \times 24$
- From step 1: $24 = 64 - 1 \times 40$

Substitute the expression for $16$ into the equation for $8$:
$$8 = 24 - 1 \times (40 - 1 \times 24)$$ 
$$8 = 2 \times 24 - 1 \times 40$$ 
Now substitute the expression for $24$:
$$8 = 2 \times (64 - 1 \times 40) - 1 \times 40$$ 
$$8 = 2 \times 64 - 2 \times 40 - 1 \times 40$$ 
$$8 = 40 \times (-3) + 64 \times (2)$$ 
Comparing this to the target equation $40x + 64y = 8$, the coefficients are:
$$\mathbf{x = -3, \quad y = 2}$$

   1. $26 = 2 \times 11 + 4 \rightarrow 4 = 26 - 2 \times 11$

$$1 = 4 - 1 \times (11 - 2 \times 4) = 3 \times 4 - 1 \times 11$$ 
$$1 = 3 \times (26 - 2 \times 11) - 1 \times 11$$

$$-7 + 26 = 19 \implies \mathbf{c^- = 19}$$

QUESTION 3D: Prove $\gcd(270, 192) = 6$
We prove this using successive divisions under the Euclidean Algorithm:
   1. $270 = 1 \times 192 + 78$
   2. $192 = 2 \times 78 + 36$
   3. $78 = 2 \times 36 + 6$
   4. $36 = 6 \times 6 + 0$

Since the remainder reaches zero on the fourth step, the last non-zero remainder ($6$) is the greatest common divisor. Therefore, $\gcd(270, 192) = 6$ is proven.

QUESTION 4A: Game Theory / Prisoner's Dilemma
1. Dominant Strategy Identification
A dominant strategy is a choice that yields a higher payoff for a player regardless of what the other player chooses to do.
- Player One:
- If Player Two chooses Cooperate, Player One gets $12 by cheating versus $10 by cooperating.
   - If Player Two chooses Cheat, Player One gets $5 by cheating versus $0 by cooperating.
   - Therefore, Player One's dominant strategy is to Cheat.
- Player Two:
- If Player One chooses Cooperate, Player Two gets $12 by cheating versus $10 by cooperating.
   - If Player One chooses Cheat, Player Two gets $5 by cheating versus $0 by cooperating.
   - Therefore, Player Two's dominant strategy is to Cheat.

2. Nash Equilibrium Explanation
A Nash Equilibrium occurs when neither player has an incentive to unilaterally change their chosen strategy. Since both players possess a dominant strategy to cheat, the Nash Equilibrium for this game is (Cheat, Cheat), resulting in a ($5, $5) payoff layout. Neither player will switch to cooperation alone, because doing so drops their individual payoff to $0.

SECTION 2 – DIGITAL FORENSICSQUESTION 10: Channels for Mutual Legal Assistance (MLA)
To submit a formal request for Mutual Legal Assistance (MLA) to retrieve international digital evidence across borders, investigators must route requests through explicitly defined international legal channels:
   1. The Central Authority: Requests must be transmitted directly from the domestic Ministry of Justice (or central diplomatic office) to the designated Central Authority of the foreign nation.
   2. Diplomatic Letters Rogatory: In the absence of an active bi-lateral treaty, formal written requests are routed through embassy networks via traditional diplomatic channels.
   3. The 24/7 G7/Budapest Convention Network: For urgent cybercrime evidence preservation, requests leverage the rapid, round-the-clock law enforcement points-of-contact network.
   4. INTERPOL Channels: Used to securely transmit background intelligence notifications and verify asset tracing locations before executing formal judicial seizures.

QUESTION 11: Fundamental Rights and the Rule of Law
The Rule of Law establishes that the state and all its operational entities must act strictly within a clear framework of publicly accessible, pre-established laws rather than arbitrary mandates.
Fundamental Rights (such as the right to privacy, dignity, and a fair trial) serve as the constitutional boundaries that enforce the Rule of Law:
- In digital forensics, a state cannot simply breach a citizen's computer network or seize data without explicit judicial authorization (like a warrant).
- If law enforcement violates these fundamental rights during an investigation, the collected evidence is thrown out as inadmissible, protecting citizens from state overreach and ensuring the judicial process remains fair and reliable.

QUESTION 12: Coercive Methods in the Cybercrime Convention
The Budapest Convention on Cybercrime outlines specific coercive powers that member nations must implement to preserve volatile digital evidence:
   1. Expedited Preservation: Ordering an ISP or custodian to instantly freeze data profiles to prevent deletion.
   2. Production Orders: Compelling a suspect or company to turn over specific stored computer files or accounts.
   3. Search and Seizure: Authorizing law enforcement to physically enter premises and confiscate data drives.
   4. Real-Time Data Collection: Ordering an ISP to intercept and record traffic data logs.

- Is Computer Surveillance Part of It? Yes. The convention explicitly incorporates computer surveillance via the real-time collection of traffic data and the lawful interception of content data under strict judicial warrants.
QUESTION 13: Minimum Rights of Anyone Charged with a Criminal Offence
According to international human rights laws and constitutional frameworks, any individual charged with an offense holds these minimum non-negotiable protections:
   1. The right to be presumed innocent until proven guilty in a court of law.
   2. The right to be informed promptly, in detail, and in a language they understand, of the exact nature and cause of the charges against them.
   3. The right to adequate time and facilities to prepare a robust legal defense.
   4. The right to defend themselves in person or through legal assistance of their own choosing, or to have an attorney provided for free if they cannot afford one.
   5. The right to examine, or have examined, all witnesses and evidence brought against them.

QUESTION 14: Legality of Collecting Open-Source Stored Computer Data
Collecting open-source stored computer data is legal if and only if the data is genuinely publicly available and can be accessed without circumventing any access barriers, security controls, or authentication checks.
- If data resides on a public website, an open forum, or a public index, it is legal to scrape or catalog it for investigative purposes.
- However, if an investigator must bypass a password gate, exploit a security flaw, or ignore explicit Terms of Service restrictions to access the data, the collection changes from open-source gathering to an unauthorized network intrusion, rendering the evidence illegal and inadmissible.
