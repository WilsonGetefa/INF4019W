FORENSIC SCIENCE
Here are concise two-line cybersecurity-focused explanations for each forensic science principle:

1. Principles of Identity, Uniqueness, and Individuality
Every digital artifact (file, device, log, or network trace) has unique characteristics that can help identify its source.
In cybersecurity, investigators use these unique attributes to link evidence to a specific system, user, or incident.

2. Principle of Exchange(There is a change when there is interacts)
Whenever a cybercriminal interacts with a system, both the attacker and the system leave traces of that interaction.
Examples include log entries, malware artifacts, IP addresses, and modified files left behind during an attack.

3. Principle of Comparison
Digital evidence must be compared with known standards or reference data to determine similarities or differences.
For example, malware samples, file hashes, or network traffic patterns are compared to identify threats.

4. Principle of Linkage
Different pieces of digital evidence can be connected to establish relationships between events, systems, and individuals.
Investigators may link IP addresses, user accounts, devices, and timestamps to reconstruct a cyberattack.

5. Principle of Probability
Conclusions in digital investigations are often based on the likelihood that evidence supports a particular event or action.
For example, multiple matching indicators increase the probability that a specific user performed an activity.

6. Principle of Progressive Change
Digital evidence can change over time due to system activity, updates, overwriting, or user actions.
Cybersecurity investigators must collect and preserve evidence quickly to prevent loss or alteration.

7. Principle of Analysis
Digital evidence should be examined using systematic, scientific, and repeatable methods.
This ensures that findings are accurate, reliable, and admissible in legal or organizational investigations.

DIGITAL FORENSIC
Here are some key **Digital Forensics Theories/Principles** with brief cybersecurity-focused explanations:

1. Principle of Evidence Integrity
Digital evidence must remain unchanged from the time it is collected until it is presented.
Investigators use hashing and proper handling procedures to prove that evidence has not been altered.

2. Principle of Preservation
Potential digital evidence must be protected from modification, deletion, or contamination.
This includes creating forensic copies and isolating affected systems during an investigation.

3. Principle of Documentation
Every action taken during the investigation must be recorded accurately and completely.
Proper documentation ensures transparency, repeatability, and legal admissibility of the findings.

4. Principle of Repeatability
Another qualified investigator should be able to follow the same process and obtain similar results.
This increases the reliability and credibility of the forensic investigation.

5. Principle of Chain of Custody
A documented record must show who collected, handled, transferred, and analyzed the evidence.
This proves the evidence was securely managed and not tampered with.

6. Principle of Authentication
Digital evidence must be verified as genuine and linked to its original source.
Techniques such as hash verification help confirm authenticity.

7. Principle of Minimization of Handling
Investigators should interact with original evidence as little as possible.
Forensic images and copies are analyzed instead of the original data to prevent accidental changes.

8. Principle of Objectivity
Digital forensic examinations must be based on facts and evidence rather than assumptions or opinions.
Investigators should present unbiased findings supported by technical analysis.

9. Principle of Reconstruction
Digital evidence should be used to recreate the sequence of events that occurred during an incident.
Logs, timestamps, and system artifacts help investigators understand how an attack happened.

10. Principle of Attribution
Investigators attempt to determine who was responsible for a digital action or cyberattack.
Evidence such as user accounts, devices, IP addresses, and activity logs may help establish responsibility.
